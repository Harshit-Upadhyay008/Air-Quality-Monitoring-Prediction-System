  Abstract

[P23] Style:Body Text Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  

[P24] Style:Normal Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:True Size:default Font:default
  Abstract — This research presents an advanced IoT-based air quality monitoring system that integrates the ESP32 microcontroller with MQ135 and DHT11 sensors for real-time measurement of air pollutants, temperature, and humidity. Data is collected every 20 seconds and transmitted to the ThingSpeak cloud for remote visualization and analysis. The system is low-cost ($25–30 per unit), energy-efficient, and accurate (5%), making it suitable for both residential and commercial use. It classifies air quality into four levels—Good, Moderate, Poor, and Hazardous—based on empirical thresholds. Compared to traditional systems, it offers improved scalability, continuous monitoring, and easier deployment. Its modular design also supports future enhancements, such as integrating additional sensors and applying machine learning for predictive air quality analysis.

[P33] Style:Heading 3 Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Background

[P34] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Atmospheric pollution represents one of the most pressing environmental challenges of the 21st century, with far-reaching implications for public health, ecosystem stability, and economic development. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 99% of the global population breathes air exceeding WHO guideline limits, resulting in 7 million premature annual deaths [1]. Particularly concerning is the escalating concentration of particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10), nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide (CO), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in urban environments.

[P35] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Traditional air quality monitoring systems, while accurate, present significant limitations including high capital expenditure ($10,000-$50,000 per station), substantial operational costs, and limited spatial resolution. The emergence of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies offers a paradigm shift, enabling the development of distributed sensor networks that provide high-resolution spatial and temporal data at substantially reduced costs.

[P36] Style:Body Text Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  

[P37] Style:Heading 3 Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Problem Statement

[P38] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Current environmental monitoring infrastructure suffers from three primary limitations: (1) in-sufficient spatial coverage due to high deployment costs, (2) delayed data availability hindering real-time response, and (3) limited public accessibility to air quality information. These constraints necessitate the development of low-cost, scalable monitoring solutions that empower communities with timely environmental data.

[P54] Style:Heading 3 Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Evolution of Air Quality Monitoring Technologies

[P55] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  The development of air quality monitoring technologies has progressed through three distinct generations. First-generation systems (1970s-1990s) relied on manual sampling and laboratory analysis, providing high accuracy but limited temporal resolution. Second-generation systems (1990s-2010s) incorporated automated monitoring stations with telemetry capabilities, though remained cost-prohibitive for widespread deployment [2].

[P56] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  The current third-generation systems leverage IoT architectures, combining low-cost sensors with wireless connectivity and cloud computing. Research by [3] demonstrated the feasibility of Arduino-based systems for particulate matter monitoring, achieving correlation co-efficient of 0.85 with reference instruments. Similarly, [4] implemented a LoRaWAN-based network achieving 95% data transmission reliability over urban environments.

[P57] Style:Body Text Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  

[P58] Style:Heading 3 Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Sensor Technology Advancements

[P59] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors, particularly the MQ series, have emerged as the dominant technology for low-cost gas monitoring due to their sensitivity, stability, and cost-effectiveness. Research by [5] established calibration protocols for MQ-135 sensors, demonstrating reliable detection of CO2, NH3, and NOx at concentrations relevant to environmental monitoring.

[P60] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Temperature and humidity compensation represents a critical consideration in MOS sensor applications. Studies by [6] developed multivariate correction algorithms that reduced humidity-induced errors from 25% to 8% in field deployments.

[P95] Style:Heading 3 Align:None Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  Data Processing Algorithm

[P96] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:True Size:default Font:default
  The air quality classification algorithm works by first converting the raw sensor reading (ranging from 0 to 4095) into a normalized value between 0 and 100. This value is then adjusted using temperature and humidity to get a compensated reading. Based on this compensated value, the air quality is categorized into four levels: if it is below 40, the air quality is considered Good; if it is between 40 and 70, it is Moderate; if it falls between 70 and 90, it is Poor;

[P97] Style:Body Text Align:JUSTIFY (3) Bold:False Size:default Font:default
  

[P98] Style:Body Text Align:None Bold:True Size:default Font:default
  and if it is 90 or above, it is classified as Hazardous. This multi-threshold approach helps in accurately assessing air quality under different environmental conditions.
